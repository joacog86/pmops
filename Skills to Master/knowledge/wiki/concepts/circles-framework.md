# CIRCLES Framework

**Type:** Concept — PM Design Framework / Interview Structure
**Created by:** Lewis Lin (*Decode and Conquer*)
**Sources:** [[sources/circles-framework-product-school]]

## One-line definition

A 7-step end-to-end framework for product design thinking: Comprehend → Identify → Report → Cut → List → Evaluate → Summarize.

## The Steps (quick reference)

| Step | Core question |
|---|---|
| **C**omprehend | What's the goal? What are the constraints? What's the context? |
| **I**dentify | Who exactly is the customer? |
| **R**eport | What do they specifically need? |
| **C**ut | Which needs are most important to address, and why? |
| **L**ist | What are all the possible solutions? (aim for 10+) |
| **E**valuate | What are the trade-offs of each? Which risks are acceptable? |
| **S**ummarize | What's the recommendation, and why does it fit the user + business? |

## When to use it

- **Product design interviews:** Gives a complete structure that hits every dimension interviewers look for.
- **Real product decisions:** Useful as a pre-mortem checklist — did you skip any of these steps before committing to a direction?
- **Stakeholder alignment docs:** The S step (Summarize) maps well to a one-pager or decision memo format.

## Key insight

Most PMs are strong at "List" and weak at "Cut" and "Evaluate." The framework forces you to explicitly prioritize and defend trade-offs — which is the actual skill being tested in senior PM interviews.

## Relationship to other frameworks

- **Ben Erez PS framework:** More depth on "I" (segmentation) and "R" (problem identification). Use Erez inside CIRCLES' I and R steps for a complete answer.
- **RICE:** A method to execute the "C (Cut)" step.
- **JTBD:** Informs what "R (Report)" needs to surface — functional + emotional + social jobs.
- **MVIP/STAR++:** Behavioral interview frameworks — different use case, not substitutes.

## Common failure modes

1. Rushing through "I" and "R" with generic user descriptions
2. Skipping the brainstorm in "L" — fewer ideas = worse solutions
3. Treating "E" as a formality instead of a real trade-off analysis
4. A weak "S" that restates instead of recommends
